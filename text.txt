  <section id="tax">
    <div class="wrap">
      <p class="eyebrow">The tax advantage</p>
      <h2>Why most of this income isn’t taxed yet</h2>
      <p class="lede" style="margin-top:12px">Most of what these funds pay is <b>return of capital</b>, not income. Canada doesn’t tax it when you receive it. Compared with a salary of the same size, far more of each payout stays in your pocket.</p>

      <div class="arts">
        <details class="card art"><summary><span class="tag">Tax</span><h3>What is return of capital, and why it matters</h3><span class="tlink">Read →</span></summary>
          <p>When an income ETF pays you, part of the payout can be classed as <b>return of capital</b>. For tax purposes it is treated as some of your own money coming back, so it isn’t taxed in the year you receive it.</p>
          <p>Instead, it lowers your <b>adjusted cost base</b>, the price you’re considered to have paid. Buy at $10, receive $1 of return of capital, and your cost base becomes $9. When you sell, the gain is measured from $9, and only half of a capital gain is taxable. If the cost base ever reaches zero, further return of capital is taxed as a capital gain right away.</p>
          <p>The result: tax is <b>deferred</b>, not cancelled, and it’s taxed at a lower rate when it comes. The mix changes every year, so keep your T3 slips and track your cost base.</p>
        </details>
        <details class="card art"><summary><span class="tag">Strategy</span><h3>Income or growth? HHIS versus XEQT</h3><span class="tlink">Read →</span></summary>
          <p><b>HHIS</b> holds large US companies, adds modest leverage and sells covered calls. Recently it has paid out close to 29% a year, monthly. <b>XEQT</b> holds thousands of companies worldwide and pays under 2% a year, quarterly.</p>
          <p>The difference is what you give up. Covered calls cap the upside, so in strong markets a fund like HHIS usually grows less than the index. When payouts exceed the fund’s return, its unit price drifts down. XEQT keeps all of the market’s growth but gives you little cash along the way.</p>
          <p>A useful test: compare each fund’s <b>total return</b>, the price change plus payouts, not its yield. Income funds suit people who need cash flow now; growth funds suit long horizons. Many investors hold both.</p>
        </details>
        <details class="card art"><summary><span class="tag">Accounts</span><h3>TFSA or RRSP first?</h3><span class="tlink">Read →</span></summary>
          <p>A <b>TFSA</b> grows tax-free, and withdrawals are never taxed and don’t count as income, so they don’t affect benefits like the Canada Child Benefit. You can take money out any time and get the room back the next year.</p>
          <p>An <b>RRSP</b> gives you a deduction now and taxes withdrawals later. It works best when your income today is higher than you expect it to be when you withdraw.</p>
          <p>For most families starting out, and for income investors, the TFSA comes first. Higher earners often benefit from the RRSP deduction too. Check your contribution room in CRA My Account before you contribute to either.</p>
        </details>

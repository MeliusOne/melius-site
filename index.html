How income compounds

Twenty-four paydays a year

Many covered-call income funds pay twice a month, some monthly. Every payout is a choice: spend it, or reinvest it to buy more units.

Reinvest, and the next payout is a little larger than the last. Do that for years and the small paydays become a rising line. Spend them, and you have income without selling a single unit.

Illustration only, not a forecast. Payouts from these funds can be cut, and their prices can fall.

The tax advantage

Why most of this income isn’t taxed yet

Most of what these funds pay is return of capital, not income. Canada doesn’t tax it when you receive it. Compared with a salary of the same size, far more of each payout stays in your pocket.

1 · Return of capital

Not taxed when paid

In 2025, about 85–100% of the payouts from HHIS and the DayMAX funds (CDAY, SDAY, QDAY) were return of capital. You don’t report it as income in the year you receive it.

2 · Deferral

Taxed later, at half

Instead, each payout lowers your cost base. Tax comes when you sell, or once the cost base reaches zero, and then as a capital gain: only half of it is taxable.

3 · Accounts

TFSA: never taxed

Hold these funds in a TFSA and every payout and every gain is tax-free, for good. A non-registered account comes next, and an RRSP defers everything until you withdraw.

4 · Family bonus

Lower taxable income

Because return of capital isn’t income, your reported income can stay low. That can help with income-tested benefits such as the Canada Child Benefit.

Articles

Plain-language reads

Tax

What is return of capital, and why it matters

Read →

When an income ETF pays you, part of the payout can be classed as return of capital. For tax purposes it is treated as some of your own money coming back, so it isn’t taxed in the year you receive it.

Instead, it lowers your adjusted cost base, the price you’re considered to have paid. Buy at $10, receive $1 of return of capital, and your cost base becomes $9. When you sell, the gain is measured from $9, and only half of a capital gain is taxable. If the cost base ever reaches zero, further return of capital is taxed as a capital gain right away.

The result: tax is deferred, not cancelled, and it’s taxed at a lower rate when it comes. The mix changes every year, so keep your T3 slips and track your cost base.

Strategy

Income or growth? HHIS versus XEQT

Read →

HHIS holds large US companies, adds modest leverage and sells covered calls. Recently it has paid out close to 29% a year, monthly. XEQT holds thousands of companies worldwide and pays under 2% a year, quarterly.

The difference is what you give up. Covered calls cap the upside, so in strong markets a fund like HHIS usually grows less than the index. When payouts exceed the fund’s return, its unit price drifts down. XEQT keeps all of the market’s growth but gives you little cash along the way.

Look at the last year. HHIS paid out about 29%, but its unit price fell about 9%. XEQT paid out under 2%, and its price rose about 14%. Their total returns ended up almost the same.

So the useful test is each fund’s total return, the price change plus payouts, not its yield. Income funds suit people who need cash flow now; growth funds suit long horizons. Many investors hold both.

Accounts

TFSA, RRSP or non-registered?

Read →

1. TFSA first. It grows tax-free, and withdrawals are never taxed and don’t count as income, so they don’t affect benefits like the Canada Child Benefit. Take money out any time and get the room back the next year. It’s the best home for your income cushion and your income investments.

2. RRSP when your employer matches. If your employer matches RRSP or pension contributions, put in enough to get all of it. That’s free money, and it beats every other option. Beyond the match, an RRSP gives you a deduction now (which can also raise your Canada Child Benefit), but every withdrawal is taxed as income, tax is held back when you take it out, and the room is gone for good.

3. Non-registered once your TFSA is full. No limits and no rules: add or withdraw any amount, any time, with no withholding tax and no lost room. Only half of a capital gain is taxed, Canadian dividends get the dividend tax credit, and payouts that are return of capital aren’t taxed today; they lower your cost and are taxed later, when you sell. That flexibility is why it often beats an RRSP for income investors who want to use their money before retirement.

Buying your first home? Open an FHSA too: a deduction now and tax-free withdrawals for your first home, up to $8,000 a year and $40,000 in total.

Check your contribution room in CRA My Account before you contribute to any registered account.

Comparing funds

The chart shows price only. It leaves out the payouts, which for income funds are most of the return, so a flat or falling price line does not mean the fund lost money.

How to read this. Yield is either the latest payout, annualized, or the last 12 months of payouts, divided by the price, on the date shown for each fund. It is not a promise: payouts are set by each fund and change often. A “~” means the yield was worked out from the fund’s latest payouts and price, because the provider doesn’t publish one. Funds marked “New” are under a year old, so they have no 1-year return yet. Total return counts the price change plus every payout, reinvested; it is the fairest way to compare an income fund with a growth fund. The price-only change is an estimate, and the live chart shows the actual price. Cost is the MER from the latest ETF Facts, or the management fee where a new fund has no MER yet. Size is the money invested in the fund.

How this works. Each fund’s yield is its latest regular payout per unit, times the number of payouts in a year, divided by its latest closing price. Your amount times that yield gives a yearly payout. Each fund is shown on its own schedule: most pay monthly, some twice a month, a few quarterly. When your mix has more than one schedule, the total is shown per month. The two-week paycheque figure is the yearly total spread over 26 paydays, for comparing with a biweekly paycheque.

Withdraw and reinvest. A fund grows by its total return: the change in its unit price plus its payouts. If you withdraw less than that total return, minus about 2.5% for inflation, the balance keeps growing in real terms. By default we use each fund’s longest record (10, 5 or 3 years, or since launch), its 1-year return for newer funds, and 8% when there’s no record yet. Switch the market to see a strong year (+30%, like 2025), an ordinary one (+12%) or a bear year (−25%). Past returns don’t promise future ones, so check again each year.

Payouts are not guaranteed. Funds change their payouts often, and very high yields usually include some of your own money paid back (return of capital) and can wear down the unit price over time. Funds marked “Leverage” borrow to invest, so they can fall faster. Figures are before tax.
